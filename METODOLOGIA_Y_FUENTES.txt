Satellite imagery and tracks

The animation uses the Advanced Baseline Imager (ABI) on NOAA's GOES-16
satellite. The published imagery for Irma, Maria and Fiona comes from the
NOAA GOES-16 Earth Engine collection. Ernesto was processed from the NOAA
archive on AWS. Both archives distribute the ABI multichannel product
and cover Ernesto. The project used ABI-L2-MCMIPF channels 01, 02, 03,
07 and 13. Visible and near-infrared observations show reflected sunlight
during the day. Infrared brightness temperatures depict clouds and the
surface at night. The two views blend gradually around sunrise and sunset.

Full-disk scans were collected every 15 minutes for Irma and Maria in 2017.
NOAA switched to a 10-minute full-disk schedule in April 2019, which was
in effect for Fiona and Ernesto. The animation uses approximately one
observation every 30 minutes. The time displayed is the start of the scan,
converted to Puerto Rico time (UTC−4).

Small track markers show official positions from the National Hurricane
Center's HURDAT2 database. The line extends to the image timestamp. Positions
between official records are interpolated. The latest official record at
each image time provides the status, maximum sustained wind and pressure.
Hurricane categories follow the Saffir–Simpson scale. Colors follow NOAA's
Historical Hurricane Tracks viewer. The translation speed is calculated
from the distance and time between consecutive NHC positions. It and the
maximum sustained wind are displayed in km/h. The olive outline of Puerto
Rico uses U.S. Census Bureau cartography.

Rainfall estimates and gauge observations

The precipitation surface uses hourly Stage IV estimates produced by NOAA
NWS/NCEP and archived by NSF NCAR. In Puerto Rico these estimates typically
combine observations from the TJUA NEXRAD radar in Cayey with gauge reports
from multiple networks, including USGS. The downloaded grids do not name
the individual stations or satellite inputs for each hour. Stage IV has a
nominal resolution of approximately 4 km. The Puerto Rico native grid has
205 × 205 cells in a polar stereographic projection, with 4,762 m spacing
in projected coordinates. The ground spacing calculated from the cell
centers in Puerto Rico is about 3.35 km.

The map uses 332 one-hour GRIB files, 83 per storm. Hourly precipitation
in kg/m² is equivalent to millimeters and is divided by 25.4 to obtain
inches. Completed hourly intervals are added within each cell and missing
values are retained. The accumulation window appears below the map title.
An image at 7:30 p.m. can therefore show a surface accumulated through
7:00 p.m. Station totals update as readings become available.

Map points show precipitation observations from USGS gauges. Select a
station to view its location, cadence, most recent reading, gaps and source
link. A circle indicates a complete record through the displayed time.
A triangle indicates a resumed record with earlier gaps. An × indicates
an expected reading is missing. Partial totals sum available readings.
Pointing to a municipality shows the Stage IV estimate for the cell
under the pointer. Some gauge observations can contribute to Stage IV,
so agreement with the separate gauge display is not always independent.

Rainfall during Maria

Maria severely damaged the TJUA radar. The NWS outage notice documents
the use of NOAA/NESDIS Hydro-Estimator and gauge observations while the
radar was unavailable. Hydro-Estimator derives rainfall estimates from
geostationary satellite infrared observations. GOES-13 was the operational
GOES-East satellite in September 2017. GOES-16 also observed Maria and
provided the animation imagery. The archived Stage IV grids do not
identify every satellite and gauge that contributed to each hourly field.

The largest Stage IV total in the displayed event window was 61.4 inches
in eastern Puerto Rico. The hourly sum was checked against the corresponding
daily grids. Between September 18, 2017 at 2 p.m. and September 22 at 1 a.m.
Puerto Rico time, the Pueblito del Río gauge in Las Piedras recorded
29.0 inches and the Stage IV cell at that station estimated 46.8 inches.
The comparison illustrates the difference between a gridded estimate
and a measurement at one location.

Station selection and coverage

The map includes 12 USGS stations for Irma, Maria and Ernesto and 14 for
Fiona. The selection prioritizes continuity across events and uses nearby
stations with data when a local gauge is unavailable. Río Rosario in
Mayagüez appears during Fiona. Lares is shown in all four events as part
of the western interior coverage. Río Rosario, Río Guanajibo, Río Grande
de Añasco and Lago Daguey have records in parts of 2018–2022 and all four
have observations during Fiona. Central Cambalache in Arecibo recorded
precipitation between October 1, 2016 and December 13, 2019 and is shown
for Irma and Maria. Río Camuy near Hatillo is used during Fiona.

The precipitation series at Vieques A1 Well begins July 27, 2021, so the
station is shown during Fiona and Ernesto. The archived USGS queries
returned no precipitation series within Vieques or Culebra for Irma or
Maria, and none within Culebra for Fiona or Ernesto. Río Icacos in Naguabo
is shown in all four events as part of El Yunque coverage.

The event catalog (estaciones_campo.html) identifies selected stations.
The full inventory (estaciones_usgs.html) contains 40 stations for Irma,
40 for Maria, 80 for Fiona and 55 for Ernesto. It includes additional
stations such as Río Guanajibo and Lago Daguey during Fiona, Arenas 1 Well
in Guánica during Fiona and Ernesto, and Bo. Guajataca in San Sebastián
during Ernesto. Follow a station link to USGS Water Data, then select
Continuous data, Precipitation, total, inches (00045), and the event dates.
Rainfall coverage can differ from that of other measurements at the site.

Other networks can also provide observations. Examples are USDA NRCS SCAN
at Miradero in Mayagüez for Fiona and Ernesto and NOAA/NWS COOP daily data
at Culebra Hill for Irma and Maria. The dashboard's plotted gauges are USGS.

Software and provenance

The dashboard uses HTML, CSS, JavaScript and D3.js. Processing used
Python, NumPy, Pillow, ecCodes and Shapely. OpenAI Codex supported code
development and review. The processing directory documents the build,
archived inputs, checksums and verification against the released data.
